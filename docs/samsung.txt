SAMSUNG ELECTRONICS: COMPANY PROFILE

Document prepared: 12 August 2026. This profile summarises publicly available knowledge about Samsung Electronics and the wider Samsung Group for general reference purposes. Approximate figures should be verified against official disclosures before decision-critical use.


COMPANY ORIGIN AND HISTORY:

Samsung's history begins with trade rather than electronics. The Samsung Group traces its origins to 1938, when entrepreneur Lee Byung-chul founded Samsung Sanghoe, a small trading company in Daegu, Korea. The name translates roughly as "three stars". Early activities included exporting dried fish, fruit, and vegetables to Manchuria and Beijing, a modest regional trading concern rather than today's multinational conglomerate.

Lee diversified into textiles, insurance, retail, and securities during the 1950s as Korea rebuilt after the Korean War. The pivotal shift came in 1969, with the establishment of Samsung Electronics, initially manufacturing basic appliances for the domestic market under licence from Japanese firms. In 1974 Samsung entered semiconductors by acquiring struggling chipmaker Korea Semiconductor, a decision made against considerable scepticism that later proved foundational to group profitability.

Lee Byung-chul died in 1987 and was succeeded by his son, Lee Kun-hee, credited with transforming Samsung into a globally competitive technology leader. His 1993 "New Management" declaration, urging executives to "change everything except your wife and children," marked a pivot from manufacturing volume towards quality and design. From the 1990s onward Samsung Electronics became the world's largest maker of memory chips and display panels, and built a smartphone business rivalling Apple. The company is headquartered in Suwon, South Korea, near Seoul, and remains the flagship affiliate of the Samsung Group chaebol, spanning electronics, construction, shipbuilding, and insurance.


WHAT THEY DO AND CORE BUSINESS:

Samsung Electronics is organised broadly around two divisions. Device eXperience (DX) covers smartphones, televisions, and home appliances such as refrigerators, washing machines, and air conditioners. Device Solutions (DS) covers semiconductors, including memory chips (DRAM and NAND flash), system semiconductors, and foundry services manufacturing chips designed by other companies.

Samsung Display, a related business, produces liquid crystal and organic light-emitting diode panels used in Samsung's own products and sold externally, including at times to Apple, and has been a significant profit contributor, particularly in foldable technology.

The broader Samsung Group extends well beyond electronics, with affiliates in construction and trading (Samsung C&T), shipbuilding (Samsung Heavy Industries), insurance (Samsung Life), batteries and materials (Samsung SDI), and IT services (Samsung SDS). These are separately listed but historically linked through cross-shareholdings and shared family leadership, typical of the Korean chaebol model. Samsung Electronics is by far the largest and most recognised affiliate, and generally what is meant by "Samsung" in a consumer context.


PRODUCTS AND SERVICES:

Samsung's mobile business centres on the Galaxy brand. The Galaxy S series is the flagship line competing directly with Apple's iPhone, offering premium build quality, advanced cameras, and leading-edge processors and displays. The Galaxy Z series comprises foldable devices, both book-style models opening into tablet-sized screens and compact clamshell models; Samsung has led the foldable category since 2019. The Galaxy A series serves mid-range and budget segments. The portfolio also includes Galaxy Tab tablets, Galaxy Watch smartwatches, Galaxy Buds earphones, and Galaxy Book laptops.

In televisions, Samsung has for many years been the world's largest manufacturer by revenue and shipments, with a lineup including Neo QLED mini-LED sets, QLED quantum dot televisions, and premium Micro LED displays. Home appliances span refrigerators, washing machines, dishwashers, and air conditioning, marketed under the Bespoke customisable-finish branding, with connectivity through the SmartThings platform.

On semiconductors, Samsung is one of the world's two largest producers of DRAM and a leading producer of NAND flash memory. Its foundry business manufactures chips designed by other (fabless) companies, competing against Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC), the global foundry leader. Samsung also designs its own Exynos system-on-chip products, used in some Galaxy devices.


FINANCIAL PERFORMANCE AND EARNINGS:

Samsung Electronics ranks among the largest technology companies globally by revenue, with annual group revenue in recent years reported in the broad vicinity of 250 to 300 trillion Korean won (roughly 190 to 220 billion US dollars). These figures fluctuate year to year and should be treated as illustrative only, verified against official annual reports for any specific period.

Profitability is notably cyclical, driven largely by boom-and-bust dynamics in the global memory chip market. During strong demand periods, such as artificial intelligence infrastructure build-outs, memory prices and Device Solutions margins can rise sharply, at times contributing a majority of total operating profit despite semiconductors representing a smaller share of revenue than Device eXperience. During oversupply, prices can fall substantially, compressing margins and, in some quarters, pushing memory into a loss.

The Device eXperience division generally provides a steadier, lower-margin revenue base that helps smooth results across cycles. Analysts often treat Samsung's quarterly results as a bellwether for global memory demand. Readers requiring precise figures should consult Samsung's official investor relations disclosures rather than figures given here.

MARKETING AND BRANDING:

Samsung has invested heavily in building a globally recognised, premium brand, a strategy dating to Lee Kun-hee's 1990s push away from a lower-cost manufacturing reputation towards innovation and design. The company consistently ranks among the world's most valuable brands in independent surveys.

A central marketing event is Galaxy Unpacked, a keynote-style launch held multiple times a year, notably in early year for flagship Galaxy S devices and mid-year for foldable Galaxy Z devices, mirroring the launch format long associated with Apple. Samsung's marketing has also positioned the brand in direct contrast to Apple, emphasising customisation, foldable displays, and multi-device ecosystem integration, a rivalry that has at times extended into patent litigation between the two companies.

Beyond product advertising, Samsung has built global visibility through major sporting sponsorships, most notably as a longstanding worldwide Olympic Games partner, alongside sponsorships in football and other sporting properties.


CORPORATE CULTURE AND LEADERSHIP:

Samsung Electronics sits within the broader Samsung Group governance structure, historically controlled by the Lee family across three generations: founder Lee Byung-chul, his son Lee Kun-hee, and grandson Lee Jae-yong, who has served in senior leadership roles including executive chairman. Family control has traditionally been exercised through cross-shareholdings among affiliates, a structure common among Korean chaebol but one drawing sustained regulatory and shareholder scrutiny over transparency.

The company has faced notable governance controversies. Lee Jae-yong was convicted in relation to a bribery scandal connected to former South Korean president Park Geun-hye, involving allegations that Samsung supported a presidential confidante in exchange for favourable treatment of a merger that helped consolidate his control over the group. The case, spanning multiple trials and periods of imprisonment and parole, drew intense scrutiny of Samsung's governance. Samsung has since undertaken reforms including board changes and more independent directors, though observers differ on how substantively these altered the underlying concentration of control.

Beyond family ownership, Samsung Electronics is run day to day by professional executives, supported by a global workforce numbering in the hundreds of thousands. Its internal culture has historically been disciplined and performance-driven, reflecting norms within large Korean corporations, though the company has stated efforts to modernise workplace practices.


COMPETITORS AND MARKET POSITION:

In smartphones, Samsung's most prominent rival is Apple, with the two companies typically alternating at or near the top of global revenue and shipment rankings, particularly in the premium segment. Samsung also faces intense competition from Chinese manufacturers including Xiaomi, Oppo, Vivo, and Huawei, which have captured substantial share in mid-range and budget segments, pressuring margins across India and Southeast Asia.

In memory semiconductors, Samsung's principal competitors are SK Hynix, a fellow South Korean firm, and Micron Technology of the United States. These three companies dominate global DRAM and NAND markets, and their capacity and pricing decisions significantly influence memory cycles. Competition increasingly centres on high-bandwidth memory for artificial intelligence accelerators, where SK Hynix has been viewed as holding an early lead.

In displays, Samsung Display competes with LG Display and China's BOE Technology Group, as Chinese panel makers expand capacity, compressing pricing. In foundry services, Samsung competes against TSMC, the market leader in advanced-node contract manufacturing, and has invested heavily in process technology and capacity, including in the United States, to narrow the gap.


CORPORATE SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY AND SUSTAINABILITY:

Samsung Electronics has articulated sustainability commitments including net-zero ambitions across parts of its operations, renewable electricity sourcing in various regions, and participation in international renewable energy initiatives, alongside targets on water stewardship and recycled materials.

On electronic waste, Samsung operates take-back and recycling programmes in numerous markets and promotes longer software support for Galaxy devices to extend product lifespans. The company has faced periodic scrutiny over labour practices in its supply chain, including concerns over working conditions and union recognition within Korea. In response, Samsung has published supplier codes of conduct, engaged third-party labour audits, moved towards greater acceptance of independent unions, and issued a public apology and compensation scheme addressing occupational illnesses among semiconductor workers.

Philanthropically, Samsung and its founding family have supported education, culture, and public health initiatives in Korea and internationally, including through the Samsung Foundation of Culture, alongside disaster relief programmes.


RECENT STRATEGIC DEVELOPMENTS AND FUTURE OUTLOOK:

Samsung continues to position foldable smartphones as central to its premium mobile strategy, having pioneered the category and maintained leadership in global foldable shipments for several years. It has progressively refined foldable hardware across Galaxy Z generations, reducing thickness and weight, while facing competition from Chinese manufacturers and reported entry by Apple into the category.

Artificial intelligence has become a defining theme in recent product strategy, most visibly through Galaxy AI, a suite of generative features spanning real-time call translation, generative photo editing, and note summarisation across Galaxy devices. Samsung has signalled intent to extend artificial intelligence broadly across its device ecosystem, including televisions and appliances, framing connected, intelligent devices as a key differentiator.

In semiconductors, Samsung continues investing through the memory industry's cyclical swings, including capital expenditure directed at high-bandwidth memory for artificial intelligence data centre customers, an area of intense focus given SK Hynix's early traction with major customers. Samsung's foundry business remains in an extended contest with TSMC for advanced-node leadership, with continued investment in process technology and capacity, including United States facilities, viewed as central to narrowing the gap.

Looking ahead, Samsung's trajectory is likely to be shaped by the cyclical recovery or softening of memory demand, the pace of artificial intelligence integration across its device portfolio, continued competitive pressure from Chinese manufacturers, and progress towards becoming a credible alternative to TSMC in advanced foundry services. Readers should treat these observations as general context rather than a definitive forecast, and consult Samsung's own investor communications for the most current assessment.
